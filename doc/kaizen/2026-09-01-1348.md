## What the sitting was

Two mechanisms that turned out to be one.  The self-shaped mark got a
date and a gate that binds an approval to the words it approves; then
`card:questions.md`'s day one turned out to be the *same* mechanism
pointed the other way — a mark is a session saying *I wrote a rule, read
it*, a question is a session saying *I do not know and here is what would
settle it* — and both are answered by a line beginning `henri:`.  Then
the three questions that had come back "I don't know" were specified, two
of them grew flags on `compare.py`, and Henri ran 48 arms that falsified

## What went right, and why

**Henri's form beat the session's, and the tooling followed his hand
rather than the reverse.**  The rule said "only Henri's hand takes the
mark off" and a session read that as *delete it*.  He did not delete: he
wrote *into* the mark, `henri: approved`, so the record keeps both halves
— that the rule was self-shaped, and that he stood behind it.  A deletion
would have kept neither and a reader next month could not have told an
approved rule from one nobody questioned.  Then he did it three more
times, always appended inline before the closing bracket, never on its
own line — and the question parser, written to expect its own line, read
his answer as *unanswered* and told him something was waiting that he had
already done.  **Four for four is a form, not a slip.**  The parser now
follows him.  This is the division working in the direction it had not
yet run: the person sets how he works, the mechanism bends.

**He overruled the session, which is the first evidence the mark is
real.**  A session argued the clone-not-a-worktree rule needed no mark —
it records a hard measurement, not a chosen standard.  He said yes it
does.  Four marks approved on the day they were written prove nothing; a
rule a session argued against marking *at all*, overruled, is the first
sign the boundary a session draws around itself is drawn too tight.
Which is, word for word, what the rule under the mark predicts about
sessions.

**24 was derived to answer in both directions, and it did.**  Not the 14
a power calculation gives: 24 is the smallest N whose interval on *zero*
successes ([0, 0.142]) also excludes 1/6, so a silent run could say the
one success had been a fluke.  That second condition is why the number
was 24, and the run came back 15/24 — **the "1 in 6" this card had
carried for a day was bad luck on a rate near 0.6.**  A number chosen for
the case that did not happen is what let the case that did happen be
read.

**`F012` was fixed before the next arms rather than after.**  Henri asked
for the two arms first and the fix third; taken backwards, because the
defect is in what an account *records* and 48 more arms would have been
48 more bad records.  Shape (a) was picked over the tidier (c) for the
same reason: (c) silently changes the meaning of a field in every account
ever written, to repair something that only bites when the cap is hit.
The order was inverted and *said*, not quietly.

**And its gate drives the real courier.**  `compare.py` matches the words
`deliver.sh:217` writes, and a copy of another program's string is a
claim about that program.  So the test runs the actual courier past a cap
of 1 and reads the account that falls out — reword the refusal and it
goes red, which is the one route a string-equality test would have slept
through.  The fixture rule, applied before it was paid for again.

## Wrong, mine

**The gate shipped checking nothing for twenty minutes, in the commit
that marks the rule about exactly that.**  `rule_last_touched` asked git
for `path.name`, so `board/README.md` went in as `README.md` — and this
tree has one of those at its root, 103 lines long.  git said `fatal: file
README.md has only 103 lines`, the helper swallowed the non-zero exit
into `""`, the function returned `None`, and the assertion read `None` as
"no edits since approval".  **All three board marks were guarded by
nothing and the suite was green.**  The red demonstration still passed,
because it tripped the working-tree half.

No test caught it.  It was caught by asking what the instrument did with
the argument it was given — which is the countermeasure belonging to the
very rule the commit was putting a mark on: *a check that asserts less
than it means*.  Three tests came out of it, and the sharpest is that the
fixture had put its file at the repository root, modelling the one
case that does not occur.

**Three instruments in one afternoon counted less than they claimed, and
one of them I handed to Henri.**  The tally glob `*-tools.md` misses the
`-2`/`-3` suffixes two runs in one minute produce, so his run read as 19
and 11 arms when 48 had run — **under-reporting the head arm by 13 of
24**, and he pasted that undercount back believing it.  The `calls` field
counted attempts, not calls run (`F012`).  And the write-up's claim that
*neither arm ever reached `work-environment-ai`* was read off the pick
lists without looking at the read lists, where it appears 22 times.  All
three were one `ls | wc -l` or one `grep` from being right.

**I wrote Henri's approval for him.**  He answered *yes, that paragraph
should carry a mark*; the mark went on carrying `henri: approved
2026-09-01, at his "yes"`.  A mark means *nobody has stood behind this
yet*, so answering it in the stroke that places it collapses two steps
and has a session writing the person's approval.  That is the precise
move the whole mechanism exists to prevent, made by the mechanism's
author, **ten minutes after `keeper.md` was written to say his hand is
the authority these gates defer to.**  Caught reading the mark list back;
undone; both halves recorded beside the paragraph.

**I edited the instrument while the measurement ran through it.**  He
said "build the two flags while it goes" and `compare.py` was what the 48
arms were flowing through.  The change is provably neutral — with
`--seed` absent the system prompt is byte-identical, and only the account
gained a line — but *provably neutral afterwards* is not the same as
safe: a bug would have cost 24 arms of his money before anything said so.
The right move was a copy, landed after.  I did what was asked and should
have named the risk in the same breath.

## What should change tomorrow

1. **A tally handed to the person is an instrument, and it gets measured
   against the thing it counts before it is handed over.**  One
   `ls $d/*.md | wc -l` beside the grep would have caught all three of
   today's undercounts. This is the day's one lesson in three faces, and
   the third face was in a command I wrote for him to run.
2. **Never edit an instrument a measurement is running through.**  Build
   in a copy, land after.  Today it was neutral; that was luck, not
   design.
3. **A person's answer to one question is not an answer to a different
   one** — especially when the different one is "do you approve".
4. Standing: the self-shaped strand's next count is whether a mark ever
   comes *off*.  Today it gained a new face — one went *on* against a
   session's argument — and `card:kaizen-ingestion.md` is where the count
   belongs.

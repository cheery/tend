## The two guards, and why one `flock -n` is not a test

A pull is a **shared** flock the puller holds on the edge, persistently,
for as long as it wants the node.  Every other reader — the panel's
`_lock_held`, `status`, `serve`'s `pulled_by`, a test's `locked()` — asks
"is this lock held?" by taking a **momentary exclusive** `flock -n true`
on it.  Two such tests on one lock collide for the microseconds either
holds it: this is F019's family, and F020 is its start side, where the
collision does not merely mislead a display but leaves a node unstarted.

**Guard 1, the watcher.**  Its job is to serve the pulled node the moment
the puller grabs the edge.  It waited with `while flock -n "$_e" true; do
sleep; done` and served once that failed.  But a reader's momentary
exclusive test on the edge fails it exactly as the puller's shared lock
does — and the watcher cannot tell them apart.  Tripped by a reader
*before* the puller had grabbed, the watcher left its loop, re-tested with
`flock -n`, found the edge free (the reader gone, the puller not yet
holding), and never served.  The puller then grabbed the edge with no
watcher left, and — nothing having served the node — hung.  Now the
watcher does not read "held" from a lock test at all: it asks `serve` on a
50 ms interval, and `serve` decides from `pulled_by` (which errs toward
serving) whether the node is pulled and starts it if so.  It stops when
the node's `run.pid` names a live process — a signal that touches no lock
and so no test can fake — or when `run` kills it as the program exits.

**Guard 2, serve.**  `flock -n "$lock" true || exit 0` read one failed
test on the run lock as "a runner is already up" and started nothing.  A
run.lock hammer made 24 of 40 pulls hang this way.  Now serve concludes
"up" only if the lock stays held across ten tries — one success is a free
lock — and starts when in doubt, since a redundant runner is refused by
`run`'s own `flock -w 2` (exit 75).
